# Positioning and Validation Contract

Use this reference for April Dunford-style positioning, 30-day evidence plans, and customer interviews. The official framework source is [April Dunford's introduction to positioning](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning); paraphrase it and keep claims about the framework attributable.

## Build positioning in dependency order

Work through these five components in order:

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would use or do if the product did not exist.
2. **Differentiated attributes** — capabilities or characteristics the alternatives do not provide in the same way.
3. **Enabled value** — the customer outcome made possible by those attributes.
4. **Best-fit customer characteristics** — the situations in which that value matters urgently and disproportionately.
5. **Market category** — the context that makes the preceding value obvious to those customers.

Do not begin with a slogan or a preferred category and work backward. For each transition, cite supporting claim IDs and record contradictions. Distinguish a product attribute from the value it enables; do not assert value without behavioral, operational, or economic evidence.

Return a compact chain:

```text
alternative -> differentiated attribute -> enabled value -> best-fit characteristic -> category/context
```

When evidence supports multiple chains, present options and the test that would discriminate among them. A positioning statement is a downstream summary, not the evidence itself.

## Design a 30-day evidence plan

Choose one or two decision-critical uncertainties. Define the day-30 decision before filling the calendar. Use weekly evidence milestones rather than a generic activity list.

| Week | Outcome | Assumption at risk | Actions | Owner | Evidence | Threshold | Stop/pivot condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Every action must produce observable evidence tied to a threshold. Separate leading signals from decision evidence. Include recruiting or dependency lead time, an owner, and a recovery path. Day 30 must end in a named decision such as continue, narrow segment, change offer, run a second bounded test, or stop—not merely “review results.”

## Validate with customer interviews

1. State the hypothesis and what evidence would falsify it.
2. Define the segment, sampling limitations, recruiting path, consent, privacy treatment, and retention plan.
3. Ask about recent past behavior: trigger, workflow, alternatives, frequency, effort, consequence, workarounds, budget, decision process, and what changed.
4. Avoid leading questions, pitch-first interviews, hypothetical purchase promises, and compliments as validation.
5. Preserve direct quotes separately from interpretation. Use only short necessary excerpts, with consent and anonymization when needed.
6. Synthesize patterns and counterexamples. Do not turn a convenience sample into a market prevalence claim.
7. Apply the predeclared threshold and report the decision, not just a summary of conversations.

Useful evidence includes recent attempts to solve the problem, repeated workarounds, time or money already spent, access to a buying process, and voluntary next steps. None is automatically proof of product-market fit.

## Minimum handoff

Return the evidence-backed positioning chain, unresolved alternatives, 30-day plan, interview guide and analysis template, consent/privacy notes, decision thresholds, and claim ledger. If no interviews occurred, label the material as a validation protocol—not validated customer demand.
